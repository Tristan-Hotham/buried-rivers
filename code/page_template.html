London's buried rivers

London was built on top of its streams. The Fleet, the Tyburn, the Westbourne and dozens more were bricked over, piped and turned into sewers as the city grew. This map shows every waterway OpenStreetMap records inside Greater London, {{gl.km|int}} km of them, split into what still flows in the open and the {{gl.km_buried|int}} km that run in culverts and tunnels, with the stories of the rivers that disappeared.

Walk down Farringdon Road and you are walking along the bed of the River Fleet. Stand on the platform at Sloane Square and the River Westbourne passes over your head in an iron pipe. The River Tyburn runs beneath Buckingham Palace. London grew up along a web of streams flowing down from the hills of Hampstead, Highgate and Norwood to the Thames, and as the city spread over them it first used them as open drains, then bricked them over, and finally made them part of Joseph Bazalgette's Victorian sewer system.

Wikipedia's article on the subterranean rivers of London lists {{sub.n|int}} of them, {{sub.north_n|int}} north of the Thames and {{sub.south_n|int}} south. OpenStreetMap, the open map of the world that volunteers keep up to date, records many of their courses where they still exist as culverts. I took every river, stream, canal, drain and ditch it maps inside Greater London, measured which parts are marked as running underground, and grouped them into rivers. The map below shows the result: blue where water still flows under the sky, red where it has gone into the dark.

    02 · How much is buriedAbout one kilometre in {{gl.one_in|int}}

    OpenStreetMap maps {{gl.km|int}} km of waterway inside Greater London, counting the {{gl.thames_km|int}} km of the Thames itself. {{gl.km_buried|int}} km of it, {{gl.pct_buried|pc1}}, is marked as running underground. Leave the Thames out, since almost none of it is buried, and the share rises to {{gl.pct_ex_thames|pc1}}. That is {{gl.buried_m_per_km2|int}} metres of hidden waterway under every square kilometre of the capital.

    The small streams have suffered most. Of {{types.stream.km|int}} km of streams and brooks, {{types.stream.pct_buried|int}}% are buried, and they account for {{types.stream.share_of_buried_pct|int}}% of all the buried length. The big rivers mostly stayed open, because they were too large to cover: only {{types.river.pct_buried|pc1}} of the length tagged as river is underground, and the Thames, the Lea, the Wandle and the Brent still run under the sky for most of their course. Canals ({{types.canal.pct_buried|pc1}} buried) go underground only in their few tunnels, such as the Regent's Canal under Islington. Drains and ditches, many of them unnamed field drains in the marshes and suburbs, are {{types.drain.pct_buried|int}}% and {{types.ditch.pct_buried|int}}% buried.

    03 · The most buried riversThe Tyburn never sees daylight

    Group the mapped ways by name and {{source.named_rivers_gl|int}} named rivers, brooks and channels appear inside Greater London, {{rank.n_big|int}} of them with at least {{rank.min_km|int}} km of mapped line. Of those, {{rank.n_big_over_half|int}} run underground for more than half of their length and {{rank.n_big_all|int}} for all or almost all of it. The Tyburn is buried for every metre of its {{lost.tyburn.km|f1}} km; the Westbourne for {{lost.westbourne.pct_buried|int}}% of {{lost.westbourne.km|f1}} km; the Fleet for {{lost.fleet.pct_buried|int}}% of {{lost.fleet.km|f1}} km, the open part being the ponds and streams on Hampstead Heath where it rises.

    By sheer length of tunnel the leader is not one of the famous central London rivers but the Moselle Brook in Tottenham, with {{lost.moselle.km_buried|f1}} km of culvert. Moselle, Westbourne, Tyburn and Fleet together hold {{rank.top4_km_buried|int}} km, {{rank.top4_pct_of_buried|int}}% of all the buried waterway in London. Many of the others are suburban brooks that were piped in the twentieth century as estates were built over them: the Stonebridge and Cholmeley brooks in Haringey, Mutton Brook in Barnet, Saddler's Mill Stream in Enfield and Cran Brook in Redbridge.

    04 · The lost riversTen stories under the streets

    The rivers that run under central London have left their names all over the map: Fleet Street, Holborn, Bayswater, Knightsbridge, Peckham, Stamford Brook. These cards tell their stories from their Wikipedia articles, with what OpenStreetMap can still trace of each.

    From open sewers to Bazalgette

    By the middle of the nineteenth century most of these streams were open sewers. The Effra was classed as one below North Brixton by {{wiki.effra_sewer|raw}}; in {{wiki.neck_dickens|raw}} Charles Dickens called Jacob's Island, on the tidal ditches of the Neckinger, the filthiest of the many places hidden in London, and in {{wiki.neck_chron|raw}} the Morning Chronicle called it "the very capital of cholera". Joseph Bazalgette's sewers for the Metropolitan Board of Works then gathered them up. His northern high level sewer took in the Hackney Brook in {{wiki.hack_sewer|raw}}; the Effra was folded into his southern sewers; the lower Tyburn, Westbourne and Fleet now feed his great interceptors running east to the treatment works at Beckton and Crossness. That is why so many of these rivers survive only as sewers, and why some are not on the map at all.

    05 · BoroughsInner London buried its rivers twice as often

    The thirteen inner London boroughs, counting the City, hold {{inner.km|int}} km of mapped waterway outside the Thames, and {{inner.pct_buried|int}}% of it is buried. In the twenty outer boroughs the share is {{outer.pct_buried|int}}%. Per square kilometre, inner London has {{inner.buried_m_per_km2|int}} metres of buried waterway against {{outer.buried_m_per_km2|int}} in outer London. Every mapped metre in the City of London ({{boroughs.City.km|f1}} km, the Walbrook and the last of the Fleet) is underground; Westminster ({{boroughs.Westminster.pct_buried|int}}%) and Camden ({{boroughs.Camden.pct_buried|int}}%) come next, the Tyburn, Westbourne and Fleet country.

    The borough with the most buried waterway of all is Haringey, with {{boroughs.Haringey.km_buried|f1}} km ({{boroughs.Haringey.pct_buried|int}}% of its total), thanks to the Moselle and the Muswell, Stonebridge and Cholmeley brooks. Bromley ({{boroughs.Bromley.km_buried|f1}} km) and Greenwich ({{boroughs.Greenwich.km_buried|f1}} km) follow. At the other end sits Hackney, with just {{boroughs.Hackney.pct_buried|pc1}} of its mapped waterway underground. That is not because Hackney kept its rivers: it is because the Hackney Brook, which once ran right across it, is not mapped at all.

    06 · What the map cannot showRivers that are only sewers now

    OpenStreetMap maps what volunteers can see or trace, and a river that has become a sewer is hard to trace. Of the {{lost_sum.n|int}} lost rivers in the table below, {{lost_sum.unmapped|int}} do not appear as waterways in OpenStreetMap at all: the Hackney Brook, the Peck, Earl's Sluice, Stamford Brook and Falcon Brook. They now exist only as sewers, which OpenStreetMap records, when it records them at all, as pipelines rather than waterways. Others are only partly drawn. The Effra, which once drained some {{wiki.effra_basin|int}} square kilometres of south London, appears as just {{lost.effra.km|f2}} km of culvert, and Counter's Creek only as its open tidal mouth, Chelsea Creek ({{lost.counters creek.km|f1}} km). So the buried totals on this page are a floor, not a census: London has much more buried river than the map shows.

    07 · Bringing them backDaylighting

    Some of London's buried rivers are coming back up. In the {{wiki.quag_concrete|raw}} the Quaggy was put into straight concrete channels to stop it flooding; at Chinbrook Meadows the concrete was torn out and the river given back its meanders, a project finished in {{wiki.quag_restored|raw}}. The Ravensbourne has been freed from concrete in Ladywell Fields. In {{wiki_sub.lrap_year|raw}} the Environment Agency, Natural England, the River Restoration Centre and the Greater London Authority set out the London Rivers Action Plan to bring more rivers back to the surface. The Fleet, the Tyburn and the Westbourne are not likely to see the sky again, but their valleys are still there to see in the slope of the streets above them.

    08 · How I did thisMethods and caveats

    Data. OpenStreetMap ways tagged waterway=river, stream, canal, drain or ditch in a box around London ({{source.ways_box|int}} ways, {{source.km_box|int}} km), downloaded from the Overpass API in {{source.osm_year|raw}}. A way counts as buried if it is tagged as a culvert or tunnel (tunnel=culvert, yes, covered, pipe or building passage), as covered, as underground, or with a negative layer and no tunnel=no; everything else counts as open. Lengths are great circle (haversine) distances along each way after clipping it to the Greater London boundary. Ways that share a name ("River Fleet" and "The Fleet") are grouped into one river; unnamed ways ({{gl.unnamed_km|int}} km, mostly drains and ditches) are counted in the totals but not as rivers. {{source.thames_dups|int}} unnamed ways ({{source.thames_dups_km|f1}} km) drawn as extra channel lines beside the Thames are left out, so that the Thames is not counted twice.

    Boundaries. Greater London and its 33 boroughs come from Natural Earth's 1:10m states and provinces, the units whose region is Greater London. These outlines are generalised, so a river running along a borough boundary, as the Fleet does between Camden and Islington and the Westbourne between Westminster and Kensington and Chelsea, can fall wholly on one side. The Thames is left out of the borough figures. Inner London is the statutory definition of the London Government Act 1963.

    What this is not. OpenStreetMap is a volunteer map, and its coverage of buried water is uneven: some lost rivers are drawn in full as culverts, some only in part, and some not at all because they are now sewers. Tagging also varies, so a culvert drawn without a tunnel or layer tag counts as open. The stories and dates come from English Wikipedia's articles; where an article does not give a date of culverting, I do not give one either.
